\abstract
% context
{Membership determination and structural fitting for open clusters are performed by separate
tools with separate data models, so an end-to-end analysis discards each stage's uncertainty
before the next consumes it. Whether the per-star membership probabilities such pipelines emit
are \emph{calibrated} --- whether a stated probability of 0.7 corresponds to a $\sim$70\%
true-member frequency --- is, for stellar clusters, not reported.}
% aims
{We present \erotica, an integrated Bayesian pipeline that retains every intermediate posterior,
and we measure it: against injected truth, against the reference implementations \asteca\ and
\textsc{pyUPMASK}, and against the calibration standard that the adjacent photometric-redshift
literature applies routinely.}
% methods
{Membership is a multi-resolution \textsc{hdbscan} sweep yielding a per-star pseudo-probability,
refined by Bayesian proper-motion and parallax modelling with full per-star covariance. Structure
is fitted as an unbinned inhomogeneous Poisson point process --- the continuous form of the Cash
statistic --- for King, EFF and corona profiles, with the survey selection function foldable into
the normalisation. Every fit returns an \textsc{ArviZ} \texttt{InferenceData} posterior.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\emph{Gaia} DR3 turned open clusters from a catalogue of individually studied objects into a
statistical population: membership lists now exist for several thousand clusters, derived
homogeneously, with astrometry precise enough that the interesting questions are about
\emph{distributions} of structural parameters rather than about any single object.

The tooling has not followed. Membership determination and structural fitting are performed by
separate codes with separate data models --- \textsc{upmask} and \textsc{pyUPMASK} for membership,
\textsc{hdbscan}-based pipelines for large-scale census work, \asteca\ and \textsc{BASE-9} for
parameter estimation --- so an end-to-end analysis is glue code, and each stage hands the next a
point estimate. \TODO{cite Krone-Martins \& Moitinho 2014; Pera et al.\ 2021; Hunt \& Reffert
2021, 2023, 2024; Perren et al.\ 2015; von Hippel et al.\ 2006; Cantat-Gaudin et al.; Tarricq et
al.\ 2022.} A membership probability becomes a cut, a cut becomes a member list, and the list is
fitted as though it were the truth. The uncertainty that survives to the final parameter is the
uncertainty of the last stage alone.

That matters more at population scale than for one cluster, because a systematic that is
negligible against a single object's error bar is not negligible against a distribution of several
thousand. Three of the systematics measured in \S\ref{sec:recoverability} have exactly this
character: each is small for one cluster and each would be read as physics by a survey-scale
comparison that did not correct for it. The same is true of the selection function
(\S\ref{sec:a1}) and of the boundary between a cluster and its comoving neighbours
(\S\ref{sec:b1}), which is invisible to every contamination metric because the stars in question
are genuine cluster members --- of a different cluster.

There is also something the field does not report. Membership methods emit per-star
probabilities, and those probabilities are used as weights, as cuts, and as inputs to mass
functions --- but whether a stated probability of $0.7$ corresponds to a $\sim$70\% true-member
frequency is, as far as we can establish, not checked for stellar clusters. Scoring membership
output certainly is done, and the prior art is substantial; the specific missing piece is the
reliability check, and \S\ref{sec:calibration} states the claim narrowly and puts the adjacent
literature up front, because most of the surrounding ground is occupied.

This paper presents \erotica, describes the choices that are not free, and measures it: against
injected truth, against the reference implementations, and against the calibration standard the
adjacent photometric-redshift literature applies as a matter of course. Several of the results are
negative, and two of them overturn numbers this project previously reported.

\paragraph{A defect in the shipped score, and its mechanism.}
The pseudo-probability is built as a product $\tilde{p} = s \times f_i$, where $s$ is a
per-star clustering score and $f_i$ is the \emph{sweep-recovery frequency}: over a sweep of the
\texttt{min\_cluster\_size} parameter, the fraction of resolutions at which the star is
clustered at all. Neither factor references the cluster that the pipeline finally selects: $s$
measures confidence that a star sits firmly inside \emph{its own} cluster --- a field star
embedded in a field overdensity scores near unity --- while $f_i$ measures only that the star
was clustered into \emph{something}. Their product can therefore be large for a star that was
never a candidate member.

We establish this as a mechanism rather than a correlation. Let $t_i$ be the truth-free binary
indicator that star $i$ carries the selected cluster's label. Across a $3\times2$ factorial of
cluster-selection rule and score type, measured on 108 synthetic realisations with half the seeds
held out, a better selection rule improves the average precision of $t_i$ by $+0.094$ and that of
$\tilde{p}$ by $\sim\!0.000$
%<- tools/validation/benchmark_tgt_selector_robustness.py, tgt_selector_robustness.json
--- $\tilde{p}$ is structurally unable to respond to a better choice of cluster. The advantage of
$t_i$ over $\tilde{p}$ is $+0.125$ to $+0.266$ in average precision across the factorial, positive
in every cell at $\geq3.4$ standard errors, and cell-by-cell the weakest selection rule gives the
larger advantage in 13 of 27 informative cells, so the effect is not an artefact of a selection
rule tuned on this generator.

\subsection{Structural fitting}
\label{sec:structure}

Sky positions are treated as an inhomogeneous Poisson point process with intensity
$\lambda(r) = 2\pi r\,\Sigma(r)$, giving
\begin{equation}
  \ln L = \sum_i \ln \lambda(r_i) - \Lambda, \qquad
  \Lambda = \int_{\mathcal{F}} \lambda(r)\,\mathrm{d}r ,
\end{equation}
the continuous form of the Cash statistic \citep{cash1979}, with $\Lambda$ integrated over the
actual footprint $\mathcal{F}$ rather than assumed infinite.

The fit is unbinned because binning was tested and failed. Under the approximately equal-count
annuli of the earlier implementation the count per bin is nearly fixed by construction, and the
Poisson dispersion index measures $0.045$ against the $1.0$ a Poisson likelihood asserts
%<- tools/validation/king_binning_likelihood.py
--- a roughly 25-fold mis-specification.

Three surface-density profiles are available. The King form is
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma_{\rm K}(r) = k\left[\left(1 + (r/R_c)^2\right)^{-1/2}
                           - \left(1 + (R_t/R_c)^2\right)^{-1/2}\right]^2 + b ,
  \qquad r \le R_t ,
\end{equation}
and $\Sigma_{\rm K} = b$ beyond $R_t$ --- the truncation is explicit in the likelihood, since the
squared bracket would otherwise turn negative and climb back up. The EFF form is
$\Sigma_{\rm E}(r) = k\left(1 + (r/a)^2\right)^{-\gamma/2} + b$, whose normalisation
$\Lambda$ has a closed form in $z = (R_f/a)^2$ and $e = 1 - \gamma/2$ that is singular at
$\gamma = 2$ --- exactly the value \S\ref{sec:a5} concerns --- where the integral becomes
logarithmic; the limit is handled rather than approached numerically. A corona component adds a
second, shallower outer term for testing whether an extended population is required.

Because $\Lambda$ is available in closed form for each, the normalisation is exact rather than
quadrature-approximated, which is what makes the unbinned likelihood cheap enough to run over a
census. When a selection function is supplied, $\Lambda$ is instead integrated against
$S(r)$ on a fixed quadrature grid.

Priors are scale-free rather than informative: half-Student-$t$ with $\nu = 1$ on $R_c$, $k$ and
$b$, and $R_t$ parameterised as $R_c + \Delta R$ with $\Delta R$ likewise half-Student-$t$, so
$R_t > R_c$ holds by construction instead of by rejection. A Jacobi-radius prior on $\Delta R$ can
be supplied where an independent mass estimate exists. Model comparison between King, EFF and the
corona uses sequential Monte Carlo for the evidence, and the log-evidence scatter across SMC
chains is reported and gated rather than assumed negligible.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

Every fit returns an \textsc{ArviZ} \texttt{InferenceData} object rather than a summary row, so
convergence diagnostics stay attached to the numbers they describe. When a trace is archived, a
sidecar records the git commit and dirty flag, the random seeds, tracked dependency versions,
and a \textsc{blake2b} checksum of every input file.

The archive index that names a trace is an \emph{allocated identifier} rather than a clock
reading. This is a small design point with a general moral. A timestamp fails both properties an
identity needs: it is not unique, so two fits stored within one second overwrite each other while
both summary rows survive --- a loop silently loses traces and the summary table still claims they
exist --- and it is not content-derived, so storing one trace twice yields two indistinguishable
archives. The index is therefore seeded from the clock, so the archive stays sortable, then
resolved against what is already on disk by comparing the posterior \emph{draws}. Comparing files
would not work, because NetCDF embeds creation metadata and two writes of one trace are never
byte-identical; comparing summaries would be weaker still, since distinct posteriors can share a
mean and a standard deviation.

\section{Validation against injected truth}
\label{sec:validation}

\subsection{Recoverability, and three negative results}
\label{sec:recoverability}

Three systematics of the EFF index $\gamma$ are measured against injected truth. All three are
negative results, and stating them is the point: each would be read as physics by a survey-scale
comparison that did not correct for it.

\paragraph{Finite-sample bias.} With the background pinned, $\gamma$ is recovered high at the
sample sizes typical of the \emph{Gaia} cluster census, and the bias shrinks monotonically with
$N$: at $\gamma_{\rm true} = 2$ it measures $+0.142 \pm 0.105$ at $N=20$, $+0.047 \pm 0.054$ at
$N=60$, $+0.023 \pm 0.019$ at $N=628$ and $-0.005 \pm 0.006$ at $N=5000$
%<- tools/validation/eff_gamma_bias.py, eff_gamma_bias_pinnedbg.json
. A survey-scale comparison of slopes would therefore read a selection-independent artefact as
physics, with sparse clusters appearing systematically steeper than rich ones.

\paragraph{A free background fabricates one.} Allowing the background amplitude to float biases
$\gamma$ \emph{upward} even where the injected background is exactly zero. The size of the
artefact depends on the profile being measured, so no single offset corrects it; the term must be
pinned, and the null cell in which this surfaced was present only as a formality.

\paragraph{Circular fits to elliptical clusters bias $\gamma$ toward 2.} Fitting a circular
profile to an elliptical cluster pulls the recovered index \emph{down}, and it does so toward the
value at which EFF and an untruncated King profile coincide --- so an apparent pile-up at
$\gamma \approx 2$ can be an artefact of assumed geometry. The effect is strongly
$\gamma$-dependent: $-0.364$ at $\gamma_{\rm true} = 4$, $q = 0.3$, falling to $-0.092$ at
$\gamma = 3$ and $\leq 0.03$ at $\gamma = 2$
%<- tools/validation/ellipticity_bias.py, ellipticity_bias.json
. That last number matters for \S\ref{sec:a5}: the systematic can steepen the recovered tail of a
$\gamma \approx 2$ population, but it cannot manufacture one.

\subsection{The EFF index across the \emph{Gaia} census}
\label{sec:a5}

We fit the EFF profile to 407 clusters from the \citet{hunt2024} catalogue; 403 converged
($\hat{R}<1.01$, ESS $>400$, zero divergences)
%<- tools/validation/a5_census_gamma_sweep.py + a5_census_gamma_summarise.py
. Among the subsample whose radial lever arm permits $\gamma$ to be measured, the index
concentrates at $\gamma = 2.006$ ($n=109$), with intrinsic spread $\approx0.31$ and \emph{no
cluster above} $\gamma=3$
%<- tools/validation/a5_gate_collider_audit.py, a5_gate_collider_audit.json
.

\paragraph{The measurability criterion is not a collider, and we tested it.}
The criterion selects on $r_{\rm tot}/a_{\rm fit}$, where $a_{\rm fit}$ comes from the same
posterior as $\gamma$ --- so it could in principle manufacture the concentration it reports. The
marginal correlation between the criterion and $\gamma$ is $-0.773$, but this is \emph{not}
evidence of selection bias: $\gamma$ and $a$ are anticorrelated within each fit by construction
(mean within-fit correlation $0.88$), so a marginal association is expected at zero bias.

We settle it against ground truth on 192 injection realisations with $\gamma$ drawn independently
of geometry. Regressing recovered $\gamma$ on true $\gamma$ inside the criterion gives slope
$+1.134 \pm 0.063$ --- tracking; compression toward 2 would give $\approx0$. The criterion admits
99.1\% of the injected $\gamma$ range, and of the 25 admitted realisations with $\gamma_{\rm
true}>3$, all 25 recover $\gamma>3$: the criterion cannot convert a steep profile into a shallow
one. Applied to a flat-truth population, the identical criterion yields 8.7\% of clusters in
$1.8\le\gamma<2.2$ against the census's 35.8\%, a $4.1\times$ excess the criterion does not
create.

A residual bias is present and we report it rather than dismiss it: relative to selecting on the
\emph{true} lever arm, the fitted criterion shifts $\gamma$ down by $0.309$ at $\gamma=2$, by
$0.177$ at 3, and by $0.045$ at 4. A trend of $\gamma$ with $N$ survives every criterion
($r=+0.252$ in $\log N$) and is \emph{not} explained by the estimator: injection recovery gives
$r=-0.033$ between $\log N$ and bias, and the fitted bias \emph{shrinks} with $N$, so an
estimator artefact would push the opposite way. We report it uninterpreted.

Both systematics of \S\ref{sec:recoverability} act here and they oppose: a free background pushes
$\gamma$ \emph{up} (median paired $+0.413$ on these clusters), while a circular fit to an
elliptical cluster pushes it \emph{down} toward 2 --- but by $\leq0.026$ at $\gamma=2$, so it
cannot manufacture a $\gamma\approx2$ population, only steepen the recovered tail of one.

\paragraph{Scope.} This is a statement about the \emph{recoverable} subsample, 109 of 407, not
about the census. Ungated, the median is 2.585 and there is no excess near 2; the two numbers are
the same fits, nested, and the ungated distribution is dominated by the lever-arm bias, so its
median is a mixture rather than any cluster's $\gamma$.

\section{Comparison against the reference implementations}
\label{sec:benchmark}

Four design choices are not free, and each was made against a way the comparison could have
flattered us.

\emph{Feature parity.} \erotica's default configuration --- the one used for NGC 6383 --- consumes
proper motions only, while \asteca's \texttt{fastMP} consumes sky position, proper motion and
parallax, and \textsc{pyUPMASK} is configured here on the same five. Scoring two dimensions
against five and reporting the difference as a win would be meaningless, so the headline
\erotica\ row uses all five columns and the lower-dimensional configurations are reported as
clearly labelled secondary rows.

\emph{Standardisation.} \textsc{hdbscan} is called with a Euclidean metric and no internal
scaling, so parallax in mas and proper motion in mas\,yr$^{-1}$ enter the distance with whichever
has the larger numeric spread dominating. Every column is z-scored before clustering.

\emph{\asteca\ is never handed the injected truth.} Its centre and member count come from its own
\texttt{get\_center} and \texttt{get\_nmembers} routines, not from the generator. A loss against a
baseline given privileged information would be uninterpretable. Photometry is withheld from it
deliberately: \texttt{fastMP} ignores it, and \asteca's magnitude cut would drop rows and break
the co-indexing the paired comparison requires.

\emph{Recalibration is out of sample.} Isotonic maps are fitted on a disjoint set of seeds and
evaluated on held-out ones. Fitting and scoring on the same rows returns a calibration error near
zero and is not a measurement.

\paragraph{Which metric decides.} Base rates are $0.50$, $0.20$ and $0.05$ at contamination
$0.50$, $0.80$ and $0.95$, so average precision --- whose chance level is the base rate --- is the
decision metric and ROC-AUC is reported alongside it. The distinction is not cosmetic here: a
binary indicator has a single operating point, so ROC systematically understates it, and an
earlier iteration of this work was misled in exactly that way before the metric was fixed.

\paragraph{Per-star error propagation.}
\asteca's \texttt{fastMP} consumes per-star astrometric uncertainties and resamples; the
comparison is only like-for-like if \erotica\ receives them too. Folding the full per-source
covariance into the sweep improves held-out average precision by $+0.135 \pm 0.021$
%<- tools/validation/benchmark_erroraware_analysis.py, benchmark_erroraware.json
, measured against a control that matches the coarsened sweep grid. The control is essential and
counter-intuitive: grid coarsening alone \emph{costs} $0.0385 \pm 0.0114$, so comparing against
the uncoarsened configuration understates the effect rather than flattering it.

\paragraph{A false-positive asymmetry.}
On realisations with no cluster injected, \erotica's error-aware configurations select zero stars
across three seeds, while \asteca's \texttt{fastMP} selects 25, 51 and 26 stars \emph{at
probability 1.000}
%<- tools/validation/benchmark_erroraware.json, negative_control block
. Any comparison of ranking metrics has to be read alongside this: a method that cannot be wrong
about empty sky is not straightforwardly behind one that is confidently wrong about it.

\paragraph{What the absolute values do not mean.}
34 of 108 realisations return no cluster for any five-dimensional configuration, concentrated at
\emph{low} contamination --- 14 of 18 held-out cells at contamination 0.5. The sweep fails on the
easiest fields. Paired differences are unaffected because failures cancel, but a single method's
average precision is partly a measurement of that failure mode and we do not quote it as a
ranking score.

\section{Calibration}
\label{sec:calibration}

The claim here needs stating precisely, because most of the adjacent ground is already occupied.
Scoring membership output is not new: \textsc{pyUPMASK} grades itself with six metrics for the
accuracy of probabilistic classification across 600 synthetic clusters, and \citet{olivares2018}
propagate observational uncertainties into per-star membership probabilities and publish a
per-star probability dispersion. What we could not find reported for \emph{stellar} clusters is
the reliability check itself --- whether a stated probability of $0.7$ corresponds to a
$\sim$70\% true-member frequency. The check is routine for photometric-redshift distributions and,
for \emph{galaxy} clusters, has been validated at the percent level. \TODO{cite Schmidt+2020,
D'Isanto \& Polsterer 2018, Myles+2021, Rozo+2015 --- prior art to acknowledge up front, not
protection.} The claim is therefore scoped to stellar clusters, and concerns what is
\emph{reported} rather than what is possible: calibration is computable for any method that emits
membership probabilities, and we compute it for the baselines too.

We report reliability diagrams, the Hosmer--Lemeshow statistic, the Brier score with its Murphy
decomposition, expected and maximum calibration error, and isotonic and Platt recalibration
returning a fitted callable --- on \texttt{numpy}, \texttt{scipy} and \texttt{scikit-learn} alone,
so they apply to any pipeline's output.

\paragraph{Why resolution and not reliability is the criterion.} Under the Murphy decomposition,
$\mathrm{Brier} = \mathrm{reliability} - \mathrm{resolution} + \mathrm{uncertainty}$. Reliability
is repairable by any monotone recalibration; resolution is not, because no monotone map can create
discrimination a score does not contain. A candidate score is therefore adopted on its resolution
\emph{after} out-of-sample isotonic recalibration, not on its raw calibration error.

\paragraph{A binning defect worth reporting, because it inverts the verdict.} Computing the
decomposition requires binning the forecast, and scores with a large point mass --- which every
sweep-based membership score has, since unrecovered stars share one value --- break naive quantile
binning: an edge inside the mass produces bins that differ only by tie-breaking. Separating the
mass at zero is \emph{not} sufficient, and this is the trap: after isotonic recalibration the mass
is no longer at zero, because isotonic maps that whole group to its empirical rate, a small
positive constant. Quantile edges then collapse, the decomposition falls back to a single bin, and
resolution --- a between-bin variance --- is identically zero by construction. In our first run
this reported exactly $0.0000$ for the strongest scores, including a binary indicator whose
average precision was $0.78$ and whose resolution therefore cannot be zero. Read at face value it
would have rejected the best candidate on the criterion chosen to decide adoption. Atoms must be
identified by \emph{mass} rather than by value; for a piecewise-constant forecast, which isotonic
output always is, each level becomes its own bin and the decomposition is exact.

\paragraph{Why resolution and not reliability is the criterion.}
Under the Murphy decomposition, $\mathrm{Brier} = \mathrm{reliability} - \mathrm{resolution} +
\mathrm{uncertainty}$. Reliability is repairable by any monotone recalibration; resolution is
not, because no monotone map can create discrimination a score does not contain. A candidate
score is therefore adopted on its resolution after out-of-sample isotonic recalibration, not on
its raw calibration error.

\section{Application: NGC 6383}
\label{sec:ngc6383}

\subsection{Does the selection function change the core radius? Two corrections that cancel}
\label{sec:a1}

Folding the survey selection function into the likelihood normalisation suppresses the core by
$2.37\%$ within $R_c$ --- measured with an adaptive \texttt{patch} map, not the all-sky product,
which at order 10 under-reads the real $0.32$ mag central depression \emph{six-fold}
%<- tools/validation/a1_patch_selection_function.py, a1_patch_selection_function.json
. Acting alone it would shift $R_c$ by $-0.033'$. But the published parallax clip acts on the same
stars in the opposite direction, \emph{over}-retaining the core by $9.97\%$
%<- tools/validation/a1_pipeline_selection.py, a1_pipeline_selection.json
, and the two selections act in series, so their efficiencies multiply. Composed correctly,
$\Delta R_c = +0.0016'$, or $0.008\sigma$
%<- tools/validation/a1_selection_corrected_rdp.py, a1_selection_corrected_rdp.json
. The published value is unchanged, and reporting either correction on its own would have
announced a revision that the other half of the physics undoes.

\paragraph{A bound, not a resolving null.} No selection map obtainable for this cluster resolves
$R_c = 1.38'$: the adaptive scheme reaches $3.36'$ inside $R_c$ at its default subdivision
threshold, and the more aggressive setting that does reach $1.73'$ places only nine pixels inside
$R_c$ --- where the core magnitude limit's \emph{scatter} doubles while its mean barely moves, the
signature of small-sample noise rather than structure. We therefore report a bound over
$1.73$--$3.4'$ rather than a null. This distinction is not pedantry: a map coarser than the
structure it is asked about returns "flat", and flat means \emph{invisible}, not \emph{absent}.

The methodological point generalises. $R_c$ responds to the \emph{shape} of the efficiency within
a few core radii, not to its aperture-averaged value: the survey curve varies there and moves
$R_c$ $1.6\times$ more than an aperture argument predicts, while the pipeline curve is flat below
its innermost bin and moves it $4\times$ less.

\subsection{The population beyond the Jacobi radius belongs to the neighbours}
\label{sec:b1}

$43\%$ of high-probability members lie outside the Jacobi radius, a figure that invites reading as
an unbound corona. Three measurements say otherwise, and the first retires the figure itself.

On realisations with no cluster injected, the membership selects $70.8\%$ of its stars beyond
$r_J$ against a $70.7\%$ area fraction
%<- tools/validation/b1_beyond_jacobi.py, b1_beyond_jacobi.json
--- so $43\%$ is \emph{below} what pure field produces. It is an area statistic, not a halo
statistic. Second, contamination is excluded on its own terms: a target--decoy false-discovery
proportion, transplanted from proteomics, measures $0.038$ beyond $r_J$, flat to falling with
radius, against the $\sim$$0.20$ at which \citet{pang2022} report an artificial halo appearing. A
manufactured halo produces a \emph{rising} profile; this is the opposite shape. Third, the
pipeline does not manufacture halos at all: a truncated synthetic cluster matched in proper-motion
centroid, dispersion, parallax and photometry recovers its members with contamination $0.000$ and
\emph{none} beyond $r_J$.

What the outer population is becomes visible only on an axis the membership never used. Of the
271 members beyond $r_J$, \textbf{179 ($66.1\%$) fall inside catalogued comoving clusters} ---
Antalova 2 and Theia 1645, both within $0.011$ mas in parallax and $0.55$ mas\,yr$^{-1}$ in proper
motion --- against a $33.3\%$ expectation from catalogue coverage ($p = 4.6\times10^{-28}$). On the
untouched photometric axis they are indistinguishable from the inner cluster sequence
($p = 0.18$) and far from field ($p = 1.8\times10^{-33}$): real young stars of the same complex,
admitted by a proper-motion-only membership. Their proper-motion coherence is \emph{not} offered as
evidence, since it is the property they were selected for.

The consequence for structural fitting is that $R_t$ is dragged past $r_J$ by a
\textbf{membership-scope} problem rather than a profile-model one, and no choice of $\Sigma(r)$
repairs it. It also sharpens the question a cluster-membership method has to answer: not "is this
star field contamination?", which a decoy null can settle, but "does this star belong to \emph{this}
cluster or to its comoving sibling?", which no contamination metric can flag, because such stars
are genuine cluster members of a different cluster.
