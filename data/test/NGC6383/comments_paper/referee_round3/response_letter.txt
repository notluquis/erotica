# Response to the referee, round 3

We thank the referee for the positive assessment of the revised manuscript and for the two remaining suggestions, both of which we have adopted. This round involves no restructuring, so all section, table, and figure numbers are unchanged from the previous version. The changes are highlighted in the marked-change PDF.

## Point 1. The R_V value behind A_V (Table 1, Fig. B.4; Sect. 6.1)

The isochrone fit adopted a fixed total-to-selective extinction ratio R_V = 3.1, with the Cardelli, Clayton & Mathis (1989) extinction law and the optical coefficients of O'Donnell (1994), as implemented in ASteCA. The previous version did not state this. Sect. 6.1 now gives R_V and the extinction law; the footnote of Table 1 refers to it, and the caption of Fig. B.4 states the adopted R_V.

To make the comparison with the literature color excesses of Table A.1 direct, Sect. 6.1 also gives the corresponding color excess, E(B-V) = A_V/R_V = 0.40 +/- 0.08 mag, where the +/- inherits the ensemble spread of A_V. It lies within the 0.26-0.51 mag range of the literature determinations compiled in Table A.1: above most of those published up to 2019 (0.26-0.35 mag), consistent with the 0.38-0.47 mag of the three most recent (He et al. 2022; Hunt & Reffert 2024; Pulgar-Escobar & Henriquez-Salgado 2024), and below the 0.51 +/- 0.03 mag of Aidelman et al. (2018).

## Point 2. The theoretical PMS models behind the Sagitta ages (Sect. 6.2)

No theoretical PMS evolutionary model enters Sagitta directly: its age regressor is trained on empirical labels (McBride et al. 2021). Because this answers the question only one level down, we traced the labels to the models underneath them, and Sect. 6.2 now summarizes that chain. In full:

1. Most labels are the mean ages of the Gaia moving groups of Kounkel et al. (2020), inferred with their Auriga neural network.

2. Auriga was trained on real-cluster photometry, augmented with copies of that photometry shifted in distance and reddening rather than with synthetic isochrones. The ages of those clusters come from:
   - the catalog of Kounkel & Covey (2019), whose ages come from BASE-9 fits with the PARSEC isochrones (Marigo et al. 2017), which in that work cannot return ages below log(age/yr) = 7.4, and otherwise from a network trained on MWSC cluster ages and on synthetic clusters built from PARSEC;
   - Bossini et al. (2019), with PARSEC v1.2S, for clusters older than 10 Myr only;
   - Liu & Pang (2019), with the Padova/PARSEC isochrones of Marigo et al. (2017), with clusters younger than 10 Myr excluded from the Auriga training set;
   - the MWSC catalog (Kharchenko et al. 2012, 2013), with the Padova isochrones of Marigo et al. (2008) for the main sequence, Pisa models for late-K and M stars, and the pre-main-sequence models of Siess et al. (2000).
   Below about 10 Myr, the model layers that can be identified in Auriga's training ages are therefore PARSEC and Siess et al. (2000).

3. Because Auriga favors the oldest stars in a region and so overestimates the ages of its younger members (McBride et al. 2021), well-studied star-forming regions were relabeled with literature ages: 31 of the 32 regions in Table 1 of McBride et al. (2021) take their ages from the Handbook of Star Forming Regions (2008), together with individual-star ages from 14 literature catalogs and, for Orion, the group ages of Kounkel et al. (2018), themselves based on PARSEC-COLIBRI isochrones with empirical offsets. The Handbook ages rest on different models from region to region; for NGC 2264, for example, the chapter by Dahm (2008) quotes, from a single study, median ages from 0.9 to 4.3 Myr depending on the PMS tracks adopted.

The Sagitta age scale below about 10 Myr is therefore a literature-anchored compilation that cannot be reduced to a single set of theoretical models, and it is not the MIST v1.2 scale of our isochrone fit. It has, however, been calibrated empirically against several grids: in the Orion Complex (a region that is itself part of the Sagitta training set), Kounkel et al. (2023, their Appendix A) find Sagitta ages 0.14 dex older than MIST, 0.17 dex older than PARSEC, and closest to Baraffe et al. (2015) (-0.05 dex) and to the SPOTS models with 17% spot coverage (+0.01 dex). Sect. 6.2 now gives these offsets. Only the Marigo et al. (2017), Kharchenko et al. (2013), Siess et al. (2000), Baraffe et al. (2015), SPOTS and Kounkel et al. (2023) links are cited in the manuscript, to keep the paragraph proportionate; the remaining sources above are listed here for completeness.

Tracing this provenance showed four things that bear on how the manuscript used Sagitta, and we have corrected the text for all four:

(a) NGC 6383 is itself one of the regions relabeled in the Sagitta training set, with an age of 2 Myr after Rauw & De Becker (2008) (McBride et al. 2021, their Table 1). That chapter reviews the 1.7 +/- 0.4 Myr derived by FitzGerald et al. (1978) for eight pre-main-sequence stars in the cluster core with the pre-main-sequence isochrones of Iben & Talbot (1966); the chapter's abstract describes the cluster as very young (about 2 Myr) without attaching that value to a study, and our reading is that it corresponds to the FitzGerald et al. value. Sect. 6.2 now states the training label and that the median Sagitta age of our PMS candidates, log(age/yr) = 6.75, lies 0.45 dex above it, so these ages do not simply reproduce the label.

(b) Sect. 6.1 described the median Sagitta age, 0.2 dex above the isochrone mode, as one of the "independent indicators" of an older age, and Sect. 3.1 said that the adopted age interval is set by "the spread among independent age indicators". The 0.2 dex offset is comparable to the 0.14 dex difference between the Sagitta and MIST age scales that Kounkel et al. (2023) measured in Orion, and the isochrones that bracket the PMS locus come from the same model grid and data as the fit. Sect. 6.1 now states both and no longer treats either as independent evidence, and Sect. 3.1 now says that the interval is set by the pre-main-sequence model systematics. Sect. 6.2 also states that the characteristic Sagitta age uncertainty, about 0.3 dex, comes from the dispersion of the ages assigned to the same stars placed at different distances (McBride et al. 2021).

(c) Sect. 6.2 stated, citing Kounkel et al. (2023), that Sagitta offers stable ages across G, K, and M-type stars within a population, surpassing isochrone techniques. On reading Kounkel et al. (2023), its coevality test covers K- and M-type members of Orion subgroups, not G-type stars, and its authors note that the result is partly by construction; the G/K/M phrasing appears to come from a later paper that paraphrases it. Sect. 6.2 now states what Kounkel et al. (2023) show, with that scope.

(d) Sect. 6.1 justified the asymmetric age interval by stating that no effect identified here or in the literature pushes the age of this cluster younger. A statement about the whole literature is not one we can support, and the sentence now says only what it can: that the model-family effects identified push the age older, and that we have not identified an effect that would shift the age of the cluster as a whole younger.

(e) While revising the footnote of Table 1 we found that it says "the remaining three" of five ensemble-based rows after setting the age apart; the remaining rows are four, and the footnote now says so.

The adopted age, log(age/yr) = 6.55 (+0.40/-0.20), and its interval are unchanged: the upper bar rests on the pre-main-sequence model systematics discussed in Sect. 6.1.

No tabulated value, figure, or conclusion changes in this revision. References added: Cardelli et al. (1989), O'Donnell (1994), Marigo et al. (2017), Kharchenko et al. (2013), and Baraffe et al. (2015).
